# Tedious Friction and Cognitive Friction

> [!tldr] TLDR
> **Key**:: Clerical labour and intellectual difficulty are different kinds of effort, and only one of them is worth preserving

Accounts of beneficial friction often treat "friction" as a single quantity to be tuned up or down. Millard's account of building an AI-augmented Zettelkasten suggests the distinction that matters is not *how much* friction a system imposes but *which kind*.

The **tedious** kind is clerical: writing out descriptions, maintaining links, adding reverse links, the housekeeping that surrounds a practice without constituting it. The **cognitive** kind is judgement: whether a concept is real, whether it belongs, whether it is distinct from what already exists. Millard reports that earlier attempts at keeping a Zettelkasten failed on the first and never on the second — the idea was never the obstacle; the labour of getting there was.

On this reading, the design question for an AI assistant is not how much work to remove but where to cut. A system that absorbs the clerical layer while leaving the judgements intact may preserve the practice; one that absorbs the judgements has removed the reason for doing it at all.

## Details

- The two are easily conflated because both feel like effort in the moment
- A practice can be abandoned for its clerical cost while its intellectual core remains attractive
- Automation is therefore not uniformly good or bad — it depends entirely on which layer it touches
- Suggests a design test: after automating, is the remaining work still the interesting work?
