# Hypertextual Friction as Intrinsic Reward

> [!tldr] TLDR
> **Key**:: The pauses in an AI-assisted workflow may be experienced as the rewarding part rather than as a tax paid for later benefit

Most treatments of beneficial friction frame it as a trade: a slower, more effortful process now, exchanged for better learning, safer decisions or deeper comprehension later. The user endures the friction; the benefit arrives afterwards.

Millard's autoethnographic account reports something different. Reviewing a system's proposed notes — accepting, reframing, merging, rejecting — is described not as a cost but as "the most engaging thing I do all day". Occasionally a proposal produces rediscovery, seeing how an existing idea bears on current work in a way not previously articulated; occasionally genuine novelty. But even a proposal that is obviously fine and simply approved is reported as worthwhile, because the pause maintains involvement and ownership of the resulting structure.

The suggested explanation is that the curatorial decisions are not obstacles in front of the intellectual work but *are* the intellectual work. Deciding whether a concept deserves separate existence, whether it duplicates something already held, whether its framing is right — this is thinking, though it does not look like thinking, because it is small and repeated. Millard connects this to Sennett's observation that craft knowledge lives in accumulated small judgements rather than in grand gestures.

If this generalises, it complicates the friction literature's cost-benefit framing: some friction may need no downstream justification.

## Details

- Contrasts with the endurance model, where friction is tolerated for a later payoff
- The claim is experiential and drawn from a single sustained practice — one perspective, not a finding
- Depends on the friction being *judgement*, not clerical labour
- The satisfaction is reported as real but fleeting; specific decisions are not well recalled months later
